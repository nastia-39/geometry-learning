\begin{abstract}
	A commonly adopted assumption in modern machine learning is the \emph{manifold hypothesis}, which claims that although data is embedded in a high-dimensional ambient space, the probability mass concentrates near a much lower-dimensional geometric structure. However, the real-world latent variables that images are drawn from are continuous, not discrete, and therefore cannot be completely captured by a finite manifold. Moreover, the assumption does not properly capture the union of manifolds, stratified spaces, and manifolds with singularities and boundaries. Drawing upon recent advances in energy-based models, we provide an energy-based perspective on the real-world latents, describing an Occam's razor style analog for the manifold hypothesis, which is more amenable to recent literature in the field. We theoretically prove that our construction recovers the manifold hypothesis for coarse functions and provide a diffusion-based perspective on it. Finally, we validate our theoretical framework with numerical examples.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

If pictures are discretized objects in $\R^{d}$, where $d=n^2$ is the dimension of the image.

We consider raw objects $f:[0,1]^2\to \mathbb{R}^3$ corresponding to depixelated RGB pictures.



A commonly adopted assumption in modern machine learning is the \emph{manifold hypothesis}, which claims that although data is embedded in a high-dimensional ambient space, the probability mass concentrates near a much lower-dimensional geometric structure.

\begin{assumption}[Manifold Hypothesis]
	Let $\chi \subset \mathbb{R}^D$ be the ambient space and let $\mathcal{D}$ be a probability distribution on $\chi$.
	There exists a $d$-dimensional manifold $\mathcal{M} \subset \chi$, with $d \ll D$, such that
	\[
		\mathbb{P}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}\big( \mathrm{dist}(x,\mathcal{M}) \le \varepsilon \big) \ge 1 - \delta,
	\]
	for sufficiently small $\varepsilon, \delta > 0$.
\end{assumption}

In practice, data rarely concentrate on a single smooth manifold. Rather, they are better modeled as lying on \emph{unions of manifolds}, or more generally on \emph{stratified spaces} composed of pieces of varying intrinsic dimensions, potentially with singularities and boundaries.

Why small $d$ is important?
\begin{itemize}
	\item Generalization error depends on the intrinsic dimension $d$ of the manifold.
	\item The number of samples $n$ needed to learn the manifold increases with $d$.
	\item The complexity of the model increases with $d$.
	\item Can get a small embedding.
\end{itemize}

Some shortcomings of the manifold hypothesis are that \begin{itemize}
	\item Doesn't properly capture union of manifolds.
	\item Doesn't properly capture stratified spaces.
	\item Doesn't properly capture manifolds with singularities and boundaries.
	\item What about infinite-dimensional data? Cause images come from infinite-dimensional continuous functions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Preliminaries}


We work in an abstract setting that captures the common statistical belief
that observed data $x_1,\ldots,x_n \in X$ concentrate on a low-dimensional
structure, even when the ambient space $X$ is high-dimensional.
Concretely, we fix a \emph{reference} distribution $\nu_0$ on $X$
(e.g.\ Gaussian or uniform) encoding our prior knowledge about $X$,
and posit that the data are drawn from a \emph{target} distribution $\mu$
that is far more concentrated — e.g.\ supported on a $d$-dimensional
manifold $M \subset \mathbb{R}^D$ with $d \ll D$.
The benefit is quantitative: greater concentration of $\mu$ yields faster
learning rates for functions $f: X \to \mathbb{R}$.

\paragraph{Differential structure.}
To differentiate functions on $X$ we fix an \emph{algebra of test functions}
$\mathcal{A}$ (a subset of $\mathbb{R}^X$ closed under pointwise products) and a
\emph{derivation}
\[
	D : \mathcal{A} \;\longrightarrow\; \mathcal{M}
\]
into a module $\mathcal{M}$ equipped with a pointwise inner product
$\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_x$, satisfying the Leibniz rule
$D(fg) = fDg + gDf$.
This gives rise to the \emph{carré du champ} operator
\[
	\Gamma(f,g)(x) := \langle Df(x),\, Dg(x)\rangle_x,
\]
which generalizes the square-gradient $|\nabla f|^2$ to the abstract setting.

\paragraph{Dirichlet form and Laplacian.}
Given any $\sigma$-finite measure $\nu$ on $(X,\mathcal{F})$, the carré du
champ integrates to a \emph{Dirichlet form}:

\begin{definition}[Symmetric Dirichlet Space {\cite[§3.4]{bakryAnalysisGeometryMarkov2014a}}]
	Let $(X,\mathcal{F},\mu)$ be a $\sigma$-finite measure space.
	A \emph{Dirichlet form} on $L^2(\mu)$ is a pair
	$(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))$ where:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})\subset L^2(\mu)$ is a dense linear subspace;
		\item $\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})\times\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})\to\mathbb{R}$
		      is a symmetric bilinear form;
		\item \textbf{(Non-negativity)} $\mathcal{E}(f,f)\ge 0$ for all
		      $f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})$;
		\item \textbf{(Closedness)} $\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})$ is complete under
		      $\|f\|_{\mathcal{E}}^2 := \|f\|_{L^2(\mu)}^2 + \mathcal{E}(f,f)$;
		\item \textbf{(Markov property)} for every $f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})$,
		      the truncation $\tilde f := (0\vee f)\wedge 1$ satisfies
		      $\tilde f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})$ and
		      $\mathcal{E}(\tilde f,\tilde f)\le\mathcal{E}(f,f)$.
	\end{enumerate}
	The quadruple $(X,\mathcal{F},\mu,\mathcal{E})$ is called a
	\emph{Symmetric Dirichlet Space}.
\end{definition}

In our setting the Dirichlet form is
\[
	\mathcal{E}_\nu(f,g) = \int_X \Gamma(f,g)\,d\nu,
\]
so $\mathcal{E}_\nu(f,f)$ is the total gradient energy of $f$ under $\nu$.
The Markov property is automatic from the Leibniz rule and the truncation
behaviour of $\Gamma$.

\begin{definition}[Generalized Laplacian]
	To any Dirichlet form $(\mathcal{E}_\nu, \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}_\nu))$ on
	$L^2(\nu)$ there corresponds a unique self-adjoint operator $L_\nu$ on
	$L^2(\nu)$ satisfying
	\[
		\mathcal{E}_\nu(f,g) = \langle f,\, L_\nu g\rangle_{L^2(\nu)},
		\qquad \forall\,f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}_\nu),\;
		g\in\mathcal{D}(L_\nu).
	\]
	$L_\nu$ admits a spectral decomposition $L_\nu\phi_j = \lambda_j\phi_j$ with
	\[
		0 = \lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \cdots,
		\qquad
		\langle\phi_j,\phi_k\rangle_{L^2(\nu)} = \delta_{jk}.
	\]
\end{definition}

The Markov property ensures that $e^{-tL_\nu}$ is a Markov semigroup:
starting from any initial distribution $\nu_0$ on $X$, the heat flow
$e^{-tL_\nu}\nu_0$ converges to $\nu$ as $t\to\infty$, confirming that
$\nu$ is the invariant measure of the diffusion.
The \emph{iterated carré du champ} $\Gamma_2^{\nu}$ measures how $\Gamma$
evolves under this semigroup:
\[
	\Gamma_2^\nu(f,g)
	= \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(L_\nu\Gamma(f,g) - \Gamma(f,L_\nu g)
	- \Gamma(g,L_\nu f)\right),
	\qquad
	\Gamma_2^\nu(f) := \Gamma_2^\nu(f,f).
\]
The Bakry--Émery condition $\Gamma_2^\nu(f)\ge\rho\,\Gamma(f)$ ($\rho\in\mathbb{R}$)
encodes a lower bound on the Ricci curvature of the underlying space.

\paragraph{Effective dimension.}
The eigenvalue asymptotics of $L_\nu$ encode the intrinsic dimension of the
learning space, providing the key rate parameter throughout the paper.

\begin{definition}[Effective Dimension]
	The \emph{effective dimension} of $(X,\mathcal{F},\nu,\mathcal{E}_\nu)$ is
	the quantity $d_{\mathrm{eff}}\in(0,\infty)$ characterised by any of the
	following equivalent asymptotics:
	\begin{align*}
		\lambda_j & \;\asymp\; j^{2/d_{\mathrm{eff}}}
		          &                                         & \text{(eigenvalue growth)},                     \\
		N(\lambda) := \#\{j:\lambda_j\le\lambda\}
		          & \;\asymp\; \lambda^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}/2}
		          &                                         & \text{(counting function)},                     \\
		\operatorname{tr} e^{-tL_\nu}
		          & \;\sim\; t^{-d_{\mathrm{eff}}/2}
		          &                                         & \text{(heat-kernel trace, as }t\to 0^+\text{)}.
	\end{align*}
\end{definition}

For a compact $d$-dimensional Riemannian manifold $M$, Weyl's law gives
$d_{\mathrm{eff}} = d$, confirming that the effective dimension recovers the
geometric dimension in the classical setting.

\section{Effective Dimension Hypothesis}

\begin{assumption}[Effective Dimension Hypothesis]
	All the observed data $x_1, \ldots, x_n \in X$ come from a learning space $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ with small effective dimension even if the ambient space $(X, \mathcal{A}, \nu_0)$ has large effective dimension where $\nu_0$ is the base distribution on $X$:
	\[
		\deff(\mu) \ll \deff(\nu_0)
	\]
\end{assumption}



\begin{definition}[Curvature–Dimension Condition, \cite{ledouxGeometryMarkovDiffusion2000} (Definition 1.2)]\label{def:cd}
	For $\rho \in \Real, N \in (0, \infty)$, $L_{\nu}$ satisfies $\CD(\rho, N)$ if for all $f \in \mathcal{A}$
	$$\Gamma_2^{\nu}(f, f) \geq \rho \Gamma(f, f)+ \frac{1}{N} \left(L_{\nu} f\right)^2$$
	Intuitively: $\rho \sim$  curvature parameter, $N \sim$ dimension
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
	Examples of generators $L_{\nu}$ that satisfy the CD condition:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item An $n$-dimensional complete Riemannian manifold ( $M, g$ ) with Ricci curvature bounded below, or rather the Laplacian $\Delta$ on $M$, satisfies the inequality $\CD(R, n)$ with $R$ the infimum of the Ricci tensor and $n$ the geometric dimension.
		\item  The classical Laplacian $\Delta$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ satisfies $\CD(0, n)$,
		\item  the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the unit sphere $S^n$ is of curvature dimension $\CD(n-1, n)$ since curvature is constant and equal to $n-1$ in this case.
	\end{itemize}
\end{example}

\subsection{Recovering the Manifold Hypothesis upon coarsening}
Moreover, we show that the effective dimension hypothesis recovers the manifold hypothesis when the infinite-dimensional space is coarsened into pixels.

\begin{restatable}{theorem}{recoveringmanifoldhypothesis}{Recovering the Manifold Hypothesis}\label{theorem: recovering the manifold hypothesis}
	Let $M\subset \R^D$ be a compact embedded $C^2$ submanifold with tubular radius $r_0$, with the nearest-point projection $\pi:T_{r_0}(M)\to M$. Fix $0<\epsilon < r_0$. Let $\mu$ be a Borel probability measure supported on the tangent space $T_\epsilon(M)\coloneqq \{x\in\R^D: \mathrm{dist}(x,M)\}$, and define the manifold-supported measure $\bar{\mu}\coloneqq \pi_{\#}\mu$. Then, for every $p\geq 1$, we have $W_p(\mu,\bar{\mu})\leq \epsilon$ and for every $L$-Lipschitz observable function $f:\R^D\to\R$, we have that
	\[\left|\int f \mathrm d\mu - \int f \mathrm d\bar{\mu} \right| \leq L \epsilon.\]
\end{restatable}

\begin{corollary}
	Let $F_\delta \coloneqq \{f: \mathrm{Lip}(f) \leq 1/\delta\}$. By \cref{theorem: recovering the manifold hypothesis}, we have that $\sup_{f\in F_\delta} | \int f\mathrm d\mu - \int f \mathrm d\bar{\mu} | \leq \epsilon/\delta$. Therefore, once $\delta\gg \epsilon$, the tube supported distribution $\epsilon$ and the manifold-supported distribution $\bar{\mu}$ are indistinguishable by coarse observations. Therefore, the manifold hypothesis is recovered upon coarsening the effective dimension hypothesis.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}
	The proof of \cref{theorem: recovering the manifold hypothesis} is based on the metric space, not on any of its spectral properties. Moreover, the manifold structure is only used to guarantee the existence of the projection $\pi$, and the same argument would also follow for any measurable set $S\subset \R^D$ equipped with a measurable map $\pi: T_\epsilon(S)\to S$  such that $\|x - \pi(x)\| \leq \epsilon$, which allows the result to extend beyond smooth manifolds. Finally, the Lipschitz bound in \cref{theorem: recovering the manifold hypothesis} is tight in general. For this, take $M = \{0\} \subset \R^D, \mu = \delta_\epsilon, \bar{\mu} = \delta_0$, and $f(x) = Lx$. Then, $|\int f\mathrm d\mu - \int f \mathrm d\bar{\mu}| = L\epsilon$.
\end{remark}

Each 2d image (or 3d scene or a point cloud) can be modeled $f: \Real^2 \to \Real^3$ as a function from coordinates to RGB (or XYZ) values. That's how NeRFS work:

\paragraph{Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs).}
\cite{mildenhall2021nerf, pumarola2021d, 50730, wang2022nerfneuralradiancefields, rabby2024beyondpixelscomprehensivereviewevolution, 9710464, 10.1145/3528223.3530127, 9578751, 9577688, 10.1007/978-3-031-19824-3_20}
\emile{this actually looks very relevant. would be worth doing a deeper dive into this}
A \emph{Neural Radiance Field} models a 3D scene as a continuous function
\[
	f_\theta : (\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \;\mapsto\; (\sigma, \mathbf{c}),
\]
where
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ denotes a spatial location,
	\item $\mathbf{d} \in \mathbb{S}^2$ denotes a viewing direction,
	\item $\sigma \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ is the volume density at $\mathbf{x}$,
	\item $\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the emitted RGB color in direction $\mathbf{d}$.
\end{itemize}

The key idea is that the scene is represented neither as a mesh, nor as a voxel grid,
nor as a point cloud, but as a \emph{continuous radiance field} parameterized by a neural network.

In a similar way, each 2d image can be modeled as a function from coordinates to RGB values: $f: \Real^2 \to \Real^3$ and the embedding of this function would the weights of the corresponding ReLU network. The regular manifold hypothesis doesn't properly capture this structure that:
\begin{itemize}
	\item In a union of manifolds
	\item Has some hierarchical structure.
	\item Has permutation-invariance with respect to weights in each layer.
\end{itemize}

Also, importantly, each image $f$ is not a smooth function: its a combination of a "simple" in some sense partition of the image into smooth enough regions.

\section{Recovering the Manifold Hypothesis}
We show that the manifold hypothesis is recovered after coarsening the effective dimension hypothesis in \cref{theorem: recovering the manifold hypothesis}.

\recoveringmanifoldhypothesis

\begin{proof}
	Let $X\sim \mu$ and set $Y\coloneqq \pi(X)$. Since $\bar{\mu}\coloneqq \pi_{\#}\mu$, we have $Y\sim\bar{\mu}$. Since $X\in T_\epsilon(M)$ almost surely, and $\pi(X)$ is the nearest point on $M$, we have $\|X-Y\| = \|X- \pi(X)\| = \mathrm{dist}(X,M) \leq \epsilon$, almost surely. Next, we consider the coupling $\gamma = (\mathrm{Id},\pi)_{\#\mu} \in \Pi(\mu,\bar{\mu})$ of $\mu$ and $\bar{\mu}$, and $\gamma$ is supported on pairs $x,y$ with $\|x-y\|\leq \epsilon$. Therefore, for $1\leq p <\infty$, we have
	\begin{align*}
		W_p(\mu,\bar{\mu})^p & \leq \int \|x-y\|^p \mathrm d\gamma(x,y)               \\
		                     & = \int \|x-\pi(x)\|^p \mathrm d\mu(x) \leq \epsilon^p,
	\end{align*}
	which implies $W_p(\mu,\bar{\mu})\leq \epsilon$. Moreover, we have
	\begin{align*}
		W_\infty(\mu,\bar{\mu})\leq \mathrm{ess} \sup_{x\sim \mu} \| x - \pi(x)\| \leq \epsilon.
	\end{align*}
	Next, since $\bar{\mu} = \pi_{\#}\mu$, we have
	$\int f\mathrm d\bar{\mu} = \int f(\pi(x)) \mathrm d\mu(x)$. Therefore, we have
	\begin{align*}
		\left|\int  f\mathrm d\mu - \int f \mathrm d\bar{\mu}\right| & = \left|\int (f(x) - f(\pi(x)) \mathrm d\mu(x)\right|     \\
		                                                             & \leq \int \| f(x) - f(\pi(x)) \| \mathrm d\mu(x)          \\
		                                                             & \leq \int L \| x - \pi(x)\| \mathrm d\mu(x)               \\
		                                                             & \leq L \epsilon \int \mathrm d\mu(x) \coloneqq L\epsilon,
	\end{align*}
	which proves the theorem.
\end{proof}
