\begin{abstract}
	A commonly adopted assumption in modern machine learning is the \emph{manifold hypothesis}, which claims that although data is embedded in a high-dimensional ambient space, the probability mass concentrates near a much lower-dimensional geometric structure. However, the real-world latent variables that images are drawn from are continuous, not discrete, and therefore cannot be completely captured by a finite manifold. Moreover, the assumption does not properly capture the union of manifolds, stratified spaces, and manifolds with singularities and boundaries. Drawing upon recent advances in energy-based models, we provide an energy-based perspective on the real-world latents, describing an Occam's razor style analog for the manifold hypothesis, which is more amenable to recent literature in the field. We theoretically prove that our construction recovers the manifold hypothesis for coarse functions, and validate it with numerical examples.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

If pictures are discretized objects in $\R^{d}$, where $d=n^2$ is the dimension of the image.

We consider raw objects $f:[0,1]^2\to \mathbb{R}^3$ corresponding to depixelated RGB pictures.



A commonly adopted assumption in modern machine learning is the \emph{manifold hypothesis}, which claims that although data is embedded in a high-dimensional ambient space, the probability mass concentrates near a much lower-dimensional geometric structure.

\begin{assumption}[Manifold Hypothesis]
	Let $\chi \subset \mathbb{R}^D$ be the ambient space and let $\mathcal{D}$ be a probability distribution on $\chi$.
	There exists a $d$-dimensional manifold $\mathcal{M} \subset \chi$, with $d \ll D$, such that
	\[
		\mathbb{P}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}\big( \mathrm{dist}(x,\mathcal{M}) \le \varepsilon \big) \ge 1 - \delta,
	\]
	for sufficiently small $\varepsilon, \delta > 0$.
\end{assumption}

In practice, data rarely concentrate on a single smooth manifold. Rather, they are better modeled as lying on \emph{unions of manifolds}, or more generally on \emph{stratified spaces} composed of pieces of varying intrinsic dimensions, potentially with singularities and boundaries.

Why small $d$ is important?
\begin{itemize}
	\item Generalization error depends on the intrinsic dimension $d$ of the manifold.
	\item The number of samples $n$ needed to learn the manifold increases with $d$.
	\item The complexity of the model increases with $d$.
	\item Can get a small embedding.
\end{itemize}

Some shortcomings of the manifold hypothesis are that \begin{itemize}
	\item Doesn't properly capture union of manifolds.
	\item Doesn't properly capture stratified spaces.
	\item Doesn't properly capture manifolds with singularities and boundaries.
	\item What about infinite-dimensional data? Cause images come from infinite-dimensional continuous functions.
\end{itemize}

\begin{definition}[Symmetric Dirichlet Space]
	Let $(X,\mathcal{F},\mu)$ be a $\sigma$-finite measure space.
	A \emph{Dirichlet form} on $L^2(\mu)$ is a pair
	$(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))$ such that:

	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}) \subset L^2(\mu)$ is a dense linear subspace;

		\item $\mathcal{E} : \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}) \times \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}) \to \mathbb{R}$
		      is a symmetric bilinear form;

		\item (Non-negativity)
		      \[
			      \mathcal{E}(f,f) \ge 0
			      \qquad \forall f \in \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E});
		      \]

		\item (Closedness)
		      $\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})$ is complete with respect to the norm
		      \[
			      \|f\|_{\mathcal{E}}^2
			      :=
			      \|f\|_{L^2(\mu)}^2
			      +
			      \mathcal{E}(f,f);
		      \]

		\item (Markov property)
		      for every $f \in \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})$, the truncation
		      $
			      \tilde f := (0 \vee f) \wedge 1
		      $
		      satisfies
		      \[
			      \tilde f \in \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
			      \quad \text{and} \quad
			      \mathcal{E}(\tilde f,\tilde f)
			      \le
			      \mathcal{E}(f,f).
		      \]
	\end{enumerate}

	The triple $(X,\mathcal{F},\mu,\mathcal{E})$ is called a
	\emph{Symmetric Dirichlet Space}.
\end{definition}

The Dirichlet form encodes the geometry of the learning space via the energy functional: $\mathcal{E}(f,f)$ is the energy of the function $f$. Enforcing the Markov property allows us to define stochastic processes on the learning space: semi-group generated by $\mathcal{E}$ is a Markov semigroup.

The main idea of learning is that if $x_1 \approx x_2$ then $f(x_1) \approx f(x_2)$ for a function $f$. Dirichlet form enforces precisely this property. As it encodes the geometry of the learning space, it allows us to define a generalized Laplacian $L$ on $L^2(\mu)$ and Sobolev classes of functions on $X$.

\begin{definition}[Generalized Laplacian]
	Let $(X,\mathcal{F},\mu,\mathcal{E})$ be a measure space endowed with a Dirichlet form. There exists a unique self-adjoint operator $L$ on $L^2(\mu)$ such that
	\[
		\mathcal{E}(f, g) = \langle f, L g \rangle_{L^2(\mu)}, \qquad \forall\, f \in \cD(\mathcal{E}),\; g \in \cD(L).
	\]
	The operator $L$ admits a spectral decomposition $L \phi_j = \lambda_j \phi_j$, with
	\[
		0 = \lambda_0 \leq \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \cdots, \qquad \langle \phi_j, \phi_k \rangle_{L^2(\mu)} = \delta_{jk}.
	\]
\end{definition}

The Markov property of the Dirichlet form $\mathcal{E}$ implies that $e^{tL}$ is a Markov semigroup. Hence, our distribution $\mu$ is a stable state of the system: given a base distribution $\nu_0$ on $\mathcal{X}$, the distribution $\mu$ is the limit of the Markov process $e^{tL} \nu_0$ as $t \to \infty$.

\begin{definition}[Effective dimension]
	The \emph{effective dimension} of the learning space $(X, \mathcal{F}, \mu, \mathcal{E})$ is the quantity $d_{\mathrm{eff}} \in (0, \infty)$ such that the eigenvalues of the corresponding Laplacian operator $L: L^2(\mu) \to L^2(\mu)$ satisfy
	\[
		\lambda_j \asymp j^{2/d_{\mathrm{eff}}} \qquad \text{as } j \to \infty,
	\]
	or equivalently, the eigenvalue counting function satisfies
	\[
		N(\lambda) := \#\{j : \lambda_j \leq \lambda\} \asymp \lambda^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}/2} \qquad \text{as } \lambda \to \infty.
	\]

	Or, more generally

	$$\operatorname{tr} e^{t L} \sim t^{-d_{\mathrm{eff}} / 2} \quad \text{as } t \to 0^{+}$$
\end{definition}

\section{Key Assumptions}

\begin{definition}[Curvature–Dimension Condition]\label{def:cd}
	For $\rho \in \Real, N \in (0, \infty)$, $\nu$ satisfies $CD(\rho, \Gamma)$ if for all $f \in \mathcal{A}$
	$$\Gamma_2^{\nu}(f, f) \geq \rho \Gamma(f, f)+ \frac{1}{N} \left(L_{\nu} f\right)^2$$
	Intuitively: $\rho \sim$  curvature parameter, $N \sim$ dimension
\end{definition}

\subsection{Sub-Exponential Concentration}

\begin{definition}[Log-Sobolev Inequality]
	Given $\mu$ for there is a constant $C_{LS}$ such that the following holds for any $f: X \to \Real$
	$$\operatorname{Ent}_\mu\left(f^2\right) \leq 2 C_{LS} \int\|D f\|^2 d \mu$$
	where $\operatorname{Ent}_\mu(g)=\int g \log g d \mu-\int g d \mu \cdot \log \int g d \mu$ for $g \geq 0$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}
	Assuming $\Gamma_2^{\mu}(f, f) \geq \rho \Gamma(f, f)$ for all $f \in \mathcal{A}$ then $C_{LS}\leq \frac{1}{\rho}$
\end{lemma}

Consequences:
\begin{theorem}[Hypercontractivity, Gross 1975]
	The LSI is equivalent  to hypercontractivity of the semigroup: for $1<p<q<\infty$,
	$$
		\left\|P_t f\right\|_{L^q(\mu)} \leq\|f\|_{L^p(\mu)} \quad \text { when } t \geq \frac{C_{L S}}{2} \log \frac{q-1}{p-1}
	$$
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Sub-Gaussian concentration]
	For $f$ with $\Gamma(f, f) \leq 1$,$ \mu$--a.e., the Herbst argument gives
	$$
		\mu\left(f-\mathbb{E}_\mu f \geq t\right) \leq \exp \left(-\frac{t^2}{2 C_{L S}}\right)
	$$
\end{theorem}

The smaller $C_{LS}$ -- the better.

	\[
		\lambda_j \asymp j^{2/d_{\mathrm{eff}}} \qquad \text{as } j \to \infty,

	or equivalently, the eigenvalue counting function satisfies
	\[
		N(\lambda) := \#\{j : \lambda_j \leq \lambda\} \asymp \lambda^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}/2} \qquad \text{as } \lambda \to \infty.
	\]

	Or, more generally

	$$\operatorname{tr} e^{t L} \sim t^{-d_{\mathrm{eff}} / 2} \quad \text{as } t \to 0^{+}$$
\end{definition}

The smaller $d_{\mathrm{eff}}$ -- the better.

	If $\nu$ satisfies $CD(\rho, \Gamma)$ then $d_{\mathrm{eff}} \leq N$
\end{corollary}

\section{Supervised Learning: Learning a Function From Data}

Adding a prior $p(f) \propto e^{-E(f)}$ over functions $f: X \to \Real$ the supervised learning problem is equivalent to the following optimization problem:
\begin{align*}
	\hat f_n =  \min_f  \sum_{i=1}^n \| f(x_i) - y_i \|^2 + E(f)
\end{align*}

Note, that $E(f)$ defines sublevel sets $\mathcal{F}_r=\{f: E(f) \leq r\}$. The generalization error of $\hat{f}_n$ decomposes as:

$$
	\mathbb{E}\left\|f^*-\hat{f}_n\right\|_{L^2}^2 \leq \underbrace{\inf _{f: E(f) \leq r}\left\|f-f^*\right\|^2}_{\text {bias: how well } \mathcal{F}_r \text { approximates } f^*}+\underbrace{\varepsilon_n^2(r)}_{\text {variance: estimation error within } \mathcal{F}_r}
$$


\begin{definition}[Sublevel set]
	Given a convex functional $E: \mathcal{A} \rightarrow \mathbb{R} \cup\{+\infty\}$, a radius $r>0$, and a metric $d$ on $\mathcal{A}$, the sublevel set is:
	$$
		\mathcal{B}_r(E)=\{f \in \mathcal{A}: E(f) \leq r\}
	$$
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Covering number]
	The $\epsilon$-covering number is:

	$$
		N\left(\epsilon, \mathcal{B}_r(E), d\right)=\min \left\{m \in \mathbb{N}: \exists f_1, \ldots, f_m \in \mathcal{A} \text { s.t. } \mathcal{B}_r(E) \subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^m\left\{g: d\left(g, f_i\right) \leq \epsilon\right\}\right\}
	$$

	The natural metric in our setting is $d(f, g)=\|f-g\|_{L^2(\mu)}=\left(\int_X(f-g)^2 d \mu\right)^{1 / 2}$
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Metric entropy]
	$$
		\mathcal{H}\left(\epsilon, \mathcal{B}_r(E), d\right)=\log N\left(\epsilon, \mathcal{B}_r(E), d\right)
	$$
\end{definition}

The smaller the metric entropy -- the less samples we need to learn a function from data.

Lets consider 3 simple enough cases of what $E(f)$ can be:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Given convex $g: \Real \to \Real$ define $E(f) = \int_X g(f(x)) d \mu(x)$
	\item $E(f) = \mathcal{E}_{\mu}(f, f)$
	\item Given $\phi: \Real \to \Real$ define $E(f) = \langle f, \phi(L_{\mu}) f \rangle$
\end{enumerate}


\begin{proposition}
	For $E(f) = \int_X g(f(x)) d \mu(x)$
	The covering number of $\mathcal{B}_r(E)$ is at most $N(r, E) = \frac{1}{r} \int_0^r \mathcal{H}(\epsilon, \mathcal{B}_r(E), d) d \epsilon$.
\end{proposition}

		\text{ such that }
		\int_{w \in W_{\kappa, M}} w d \omega(w) = f
	\end{align*}
\end{lemma}

Hence, if we know the set $W_{\kappa, M}$ then \Cref{eq:s-min} can be rewritten as:

\begin{align*}
	\min_{\omega \in \mathfrak{M}_+(W)}
	\mathbb{E}_{p_T} \left[ \left\| \int_{w \in W_{\kappa, M}} w d \omega(w) - \nabla \log p_{T} \right\|^2 \right]
	+
	\| \omega \|_{TV}
\end{align*}

\begin{definition}[AND--OR Abstract Syntax Tree]
	Let $\mathcal X$ be an input space and $\phi:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R^d$ an embedding.
	An \emph{AND--OR abstract syntax tree (AST)} is defined recursively as:

	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Atom (Leaf):}
		      \[
			      T(x) = g(x), \qquad g \in \mathcal G,
		      \]
		      where $\mathcal G$ is a class of simple local models (e.g.\ linear or affine functions).

		\item \textbf{AND node:}
		      \[
			      T(x) = \mathbb I\bigl[C_1(x)\wedge\cdots\wedge C_k(x)\bigr]\cdot T'(x),
		      \]
		      where each $C_i(x)$ is a constraint and $T'$ is a subtree.

		\item \textbf{OR node:}
		      \[
			      T(x) = \sum_{j=1}^m \mathbb I\bigl[C_j(x)\bigr]\cdot T_j(x),
		      \]
		      where $\{C_j\}_{j=1}^m$ form a partition of $\mathcal X$ and $T_j$ are subtrees.
	\end{itemize}

	The constraints $C(x)$ are typically hyperplane tests of the form
	\[
		C(x) := \langle w, \phi(x)\rangle \ge 0 .
	\]
\end{definition}


\paragraph{Semantics.}
\begin{itemize}
	\item AND nodes represent conjunctions of constraints and correspond to intersections
	      of half-spaces or cross-products of independent factors.
	\item OR nodes represent partitions of the input space into disjoint regions.
	\item Each root-to-leaf path defines a region
	      \[
		      \mathcal X_\ell = \bigcap_{C \in \text{path}(\ell)} C,
	      \]
	      on which the function reduces to a simple local model.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Example: 1D Piecewise Linear Function}
Let
\[
	f(x)=
	\begin{cases}
		a_1 x + b_1, & x \le t, \\
		a_2 x + b_2, & x > t.
	\end{cases}
\]

This function admits the AND--OR representation
\[
	f(x)
	=
	\mathbb I[x\le t]\,(a_1x+b_1)
	+
	\mathbb I[x>t]\,(a_2x+b_2).
\]

Equivalently, as an AST:
\[
	\texttt{OR}\Bigl(
	\texttt{AND}(x\le t,\ \texttt{LINEAR}(a_1,b_1)),
	\texttt{AND}(x>t,\ \texttt{LINEAR}(a_2,b_2))
	\Bigr).
\]

\paragraph{Example: Hierarchical Partition}
Let $t_1 < t_2$ and
\[
	f(x)=
	\begin{cases}
		a_1x+b_1, & x\le t_1,        \\
		a_2x+b_2, & t_1 < x \le t_2, \\
		a_3x+b_3, & x>t_2.
	\end{cases}
\]

Then $f$ can be written as
\[
	f(x)=
	\mathbb I[x\le t_2]
	\Bigl(
	\mathbb I[x\le t_1](a_1x+b_1)
	+
	\mathbb I[t_1<x\le t_2](a_2x+b_2)
	\Bigr)
	+
	\mathbb I[x>t_2](a_3x+b_3).
\]

This corresponds to a depth--2 AND--OR tree.


\paragraph{Example: ReLU as AND--OR}
For $w\in\mathbb R^d$,
\[
	\mathrm{ReLU}(\langle w,x\rangle)
	=
	\mathbb I[\langle w,x\rangle\ge0]\langle w,x\rangle
	+
	\mathbb I[\langle w,x\rangle<0]\cdot 0.
\]

Thus a ReLU unit is an OR node with two AND branches.
A finite ReLU network corresponds to a (possibly large) AND--OR tree,
whose leaves are linear functions and whose OR structure encodes the
activation pattern partition.\\
